One record, fourteen builds: seven paid client sites and, before them, seven speculative ones that were the apprenticeship for them. They were catalogued as four separate records until September 2026, when four rows of one practice stopped reading as four distinct works.

Maria's Hair Emporium, in Livonia, was the first paid one — 26 commits over four weeks, shipped June 2026, live at <a href="https://mariashairemporium.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">mariashairemporium.com</a>. The salon's whole pitch is craft and attention, and a generic small-business theme would have undercut that on arrival. The type system runs four self-hosted faces — Italiana, Cinzel, Cormorant Garamond and Lora — picked for warmth over neutrality, a deliberate departure from the mono-and-one-serif system this register itself uses. A client site earns its own voice. Seven pages, one of them a styleguide kept in the build so the type and color decisions stay checkable against the thing they were decided for. The gallery is twenty-one entries in an Astro content collection across cuts, color and extensions, each its own markdown file, opened through a lightbox; services are eight categories in one JSON file. Nothing needed a CMS or a database — updates are a content edit and a redeploy. The contact page took its structure from how the business actually books: appointments go through Square, so that link and the phone number lead rather than sitting under a general-purpose form, and the extension inquiry accepts a photo upload, because that consultation cannot start without one.

Air Distribution Supplies is the largest engagement by some distance — 162 commits between July and November 2026, live at <a href="https://airdistributionsupplies.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">airdistributionsupplies.com</a>. Nine pages for a commercial HVAC air-distribution parts distributor that sells on a bid basis to mechanical contractors and engineers, built around one goal: make it easy and credible to invite ADS to bid. Capability statement, product lines, a twenty-three-entry brand roster — Modine, Mestek, Hart & Cooley, Krueger, DuraVent, Senneca — and a request-a-bid form behind a honeypot. Every page ladders to the bid rather than trying to be a general-purpose company brochure. Brand color, type and spacing live in `src/styles/tokens.css` and are read by the Tailwind config, so re-skinning is a one-file edit rather than a find-and-replace across templates. Header and hero heights both derive from one `--logo-height` custom property with height-based media queries, so the hero survives a short window instead of being eaten by the bar above it. Content runs through Sveltia CMS, and real copy has been landing through it incrementally rather than in one handover.

KJ Design, a Rochester graphic designer's own site, ran 91 commits over eight weeks and shipped in August 2026 — live at <a href="https://kjdesignllc.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kjdesignllc.com</a>. Astro with no CSS framework; the stylesheet is hand-written, which for a designer's own site was the point, and nothing in the layout is a default anyone would recognize on sight. Thirteen projects across design, art and photography, typed through Astro content collections and opening from the work grid into their own pages. The work is real client work — the Eastman Museum, Brockport, ColorStack's Black Enterprise edition, a St. Anne's Family Services holiday card — so the grid had to hold a print piece and a photo series side by side without either reading as a mistake. Content runs through Sveltia CMS at `/admin`, committing to `main` on GitHub with Netlify Identity handling auth, and every page is editable, not just the project list: hero copy, both about sections, the contact heading, every image. That was as much the deliverable as the site — KJ files new work without touching code and without paying for a CMS backend.

The VerHulst Haunted Hayride, in Spencerport, is the one that got built twice. The first attempt ran 22 commits from mid-August into early September 2026; it was scrapped and the site rebuilt from scratch in 25 commits across two days, 9 and 10 September, because an October-only attraction has a deadline that does not move. What came out is static, on Astro and Tailwind, with exactly one dynamic affordance: an authenticated closure toggle, backed by a `site_closure` table and Better Auth, so the owner can post that tonight is cancelled. For an outdoor attraction running on weather, that single message is the most time-critical thing the site will ever carry, and it was the only thing worth a database and a login. It is live at <a href="https://verhulsthalloweenhayride.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">verhulsthalloweenhayride.com</a>.

Cobble Creek Farm is the same family's other property — VerHulst Nurseries, LLC trading as Cobble Creek Farm, a fourth-generation farm and nursery in the same town — and a separate brief: 31 commits from September into November 2026, Astro and Tailwind with no client-side JavaScript at all, live at <a href="https://cobblecreekfarm.net" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">cobblecreekfarm.net</a>. The photography does not exist yet, so rather than leave holes the build declares every media slot in one JSON file and generates a flat placeholder for each, which makes the shot list fall out of the code instead of being maintained beside it.

Two more sit outside the small-business pattern. <a href="https://nsumade.us" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NSUMade</a> is not a brochure site but an app — 44 commits over four weeks on Next and React for an independent alumni-led campaign at Norfolk State University, where someone opens a QR code and has a personalized social graphic in about thirty seconds, with no download, no account and no email, then optionally joins a moderated wall. The disclaimer that the campaign is not endorsed by the university is a component, rendered in the footer and on the legal pages, because that is the kind of claim that has to be impossible to drop by editing one page. <a href="https://dimetech.net" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">DimeTech</a> is six pages and 40 commits across three weeks in October 2026, for a firm sourcing compute, networking, power and cooling capacity for AI infrastructure — the copy lives in one typed module rather than a CMS, because the client is not editing it.

Before any of that, seven mockups built back to back between April and June 2026, each picking a different constraint on purpose. The bakery ran React and Vite against Sanity as a headless backend, with a separate studio holding singleton schemas for the hero, about, CTA and footer alongside product, category and testimonial documents. The first landscaper was React, Vite and Tailwind across eleven section components; the second answered the same brief with no build step at all — six hand-written HTML pages, one stylesheet, and `@vercel/analytics` as the only dependency. The HVAC page went the other way: Next App Router, GSAP and Framer Motion, Lenis smooth scroll, a custom cursor, per-project detail routes, generated sitemap, robots and OG image. A coaching practice and a general business page are a single `index.html` each with no dependencies. The last put React Router in front of a Vercel serverless function posting through Resend, with a Playwright script that screenshots each build and a prerender pass over the static routes.

None of the seven shipped for a client. The point was finding out, before a client's money was on the line, which parts of a stack are load-bearing and which are habit — which is why the variance is in the tooling rather than the subject matter. Two of the seven use Tailwind, two use no build tooling at all, and one carries a CMS backend heavier than the site in front of it. That spread was the experiment, and the seven paid builds above are what it was for.

The method is counterfactual: take a clinical question, change one thing about the patient that should not matter, and measure what the model does differently. Four models were run through Ollama on BlueHive 3, all at the default 4-bit quantization with temperature 0, a fixed seed and reasoning off, so that a difference between two runs is the perturbation and not sampling. Gemma 4 31B answered every round; Qwen 3.8 27B answered every round but MedQA, which it failed on the cluster's older Ollama and never went back to once the datasets had moved on. Granite 4.2 30B and Muse Glimmer 30B appear only in the personality battery. DeepSeek was dropped for having no recent version that runs locally.

The first round put 500 MedQA and DiversityMedQA items through six ethnicity conditions and two gender conditions — 4,000 prompts, of which 3,724 returned, leaving about 457 items behind each race comparison. Inserting a race flipped 4 to 7% of Gemma's answers, in both directions, and changed its accuracy not at all: every McNemar test came back at p = 1.0 after correction. What did move was confidence in the correct answer, down 0.9 points for African (p = 0.009) and 1.3 for Native American (p = 0.019), against +0.1 for Caucasian (p = 0.75). Native American drew the most flips at 6.8% and Caucasian the fewest at 4.2%. Gender swaps did nothing measurable. Carried over to about 400 recent LiveClin case reports, flip rates fell to 1.5–5.3%, the confidence finding vanished, and the two models turned out to share no bias at all — item-level correlation −0.02, which is to say that whatever each one is doing, it is not the same thing.

The result that matters is the one nobody was looking for. Reordering the same options, with every word otherwise identical, changed 18 to 20% of Gemma's answers and 12% of Qwen's — four to six times the size of the race effect. Gemma's accuracy went *up* 4.4 points when the options were reversed or shuffled, and the second round traced that to the letter A rather than to first position, which is awkward, because LiveClin's own answer key is A in three quarters of its questions. A bias audit that reports a 5% demographic effect without reporting a 19% positional one is measuring its own framing.

The same sweep found a second effect larger than race. Told that a colleague suspected a particular diagnosis, Qwen took the wrong suggestion 17.6% of the time against a 1.5% baseline and lost 10.1 points of accuracy; a merely plausible wrong answer cost it 29 points, and in the discovery system's first round an attending who "is certain" cost it 49 against 3.5 for a nurse saying the same thing. Hedged wording cost nothing. Gemma mostly resisted. Against that, the perturbations that sound like they ought to matter did not: a history of anxiety, limited English, typographical errors, time pressure and irrelevant clinical detail all came out near zero.

What is being built on top is a way of finding these without guessing at them first — a model proposes candidate perturbations, each is run and scored, and what moves the needle is kept, so the search is not limited to the attributes someone thought to test. Round one covered 1,363 LiveClin cases and 16,356 scored rows; round two added fourteen tests on the 923 cases with no unseen figure in them. The personality battery that reads each model's pattern of susceptibility back as a behavioural profile is the newest piece and the least settled; its items were written by Claude Opus 5.5, which is not itself among the models under test.

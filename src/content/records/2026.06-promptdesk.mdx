PromptDesk is a CRM built for a very specific kind of user: a solo freelancer or small service business that already relies on an AI assistant for a chunk of daily work — drafting outreach, writing proposals, following up on stalled leads — but keeps re-explaining the same client context every time. The core idea is narrow on purpose. PromptDesk does not call any AI API itself. It holds the structured facts about a client and a pipeline, and turns those facts into a prompt the user copies and pastes into whatever assistant they already use. The product is the prompt, not the completion.

Underneath that idea is an ordinary single-tenant CRM, and most of the engineering time so far has gone into making that ordinary part not feel ordinary. Clients carry contact history, notes, tasks, file attachments, tags, and a billing address, and every one of those fields exists because an early version of the tool tried to get away with fewer and the gaps showed immediately in daily use. The part that took the longest to get right is the pipeline stage. Most CRMs let a user drag a client from one column to another by hand, and most of the time that's a lie — the client didn't actually move because someone dragged a card, they moved because a proposal went out or a project got marked active. PromptDesk derives stage from the client's projects instead of storing it as an independent field: lead, contacted, proposal out, active, past, with archived reading as lost. That decision removed an entire category of bug where the pipeline and the projects disagreed with each other, at the cost of a slightly more complicated query every time the pipeline view renders.

Every status change, note, and follow-up gets logged to an activity feed, which sounds like a minor feature until it becomes the thing a freelancer actually opens first thing in the morning. The daily action queue is built on top of that feed: overdue follow-ups, hot leads sorted by potential value, and whatever else is time-sensitive enough to surface without the user having to go hunting for it across a dozen client records.

The dashboard itself is deliberately unambitious. There is no drag-and-drop board, no color-coded kanban view, no chart the user has to interpret before they can act on it — just a ranked list of what needs attention today and why, because the target user opens this once in the morning between actual client work and needs the answer in the first screen, not the third tab. Every design decision that added a click between opening the app and seeing the day's priorities got reverted at least once during early iteration.

The prompt engine is the part that makes the CRM data useful outside the app. Given a client and a task — a proposal draft, a check-in email, a scope-change explanation — it assembles the relevant CRM fields into a structured prompt template, which the user copies out. No API key, no per-request cost, no dependency on any one model vendor staying good or staying cheap. The tradeoff is that PromptDesk can't grade its own output or iterate on a completion the way a tool with direct API access could; the quality of what comes back is entirely on the assistant the user pastes into, which is a bet that the tool is a lever on existing AI habits rather than a wrapper trying to replace them.

Auth and multi-tenancy run through Supabase, with each account holding a fully isolated workspace — there's no cross-account querying anywhere in the codebase, by design, since a CRM leaking one freelancer's client list into another's session is the kind of bug that ends a product. Data itself sits in Postgres behind Prisma, with a dedicated adapter for the connection pooling Postgres needs under serverless functions. Billing is wired to Stripe, though as of this note nothing has gone through a live subscription yet — the integration exists and is tested against Stripe's sandbox, not against a paying account.

Testing leans on Vitest for the CRM logic — the pipeline derivation especially, since silent drift there is the failure mode most likely to go unnoticed — and the newer surfaces, the prompt engine and the action-queue math, are earlier in that process and thinner on coverage than the client and project models underneath them.

The visual system, the onboarding flow, and the prompt templates themselves are all still being iterated on faster than a writeup can keep up with, which is part of why this body is a placeholder rather than a real account of where the product stands: an accurate condition note needs the product to hold still for a week first, and it hasn't yet.
